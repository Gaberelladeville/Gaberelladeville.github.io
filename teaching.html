Teaching

Students learn best when they are active participants in discovery. I build inclusive, student-centered courses that diversify assessment, structure peer collaboration, and make scientific practice visible, and I want students to leave thinking and working like scientists.

§ 1

Where it comes from

During my first years of college I often questioned whether I belonged in biology. Large lectures made it hard to connect abstract pathways to anything meaningful, and without that context I retained little beyond the exam. That changed during a quarter at Bodega Marine Laboratory, where we designed and carried out original experiments in marine physiology. The experiments were imperfect, but moving from question to data to interpretation rekindled my curiosity, and it shaped my belief that inquiry-driven learning can change how students see themselves as scientists.

It is also why I treat belonging as something a course design either builds or erodes. Students decide early whether science is for them, partly from evidence we control: whether the work is legible, whether early mistakes are survivable, and whether their questions get taken seriously. I find introductory and non-majors courses as exciting as advanced ones for exactly this reason. They are where curiosity gets ignited or not.

§ 2

What I actually do in the room

I rely on a small set of strategies with good empirical support, and I use them consistently rather than occasionally.

  • Open a unit with a question, not a definition. Students meeting membrane potential for the first time begin with why a neuron in a warming tide pool starts to misfire, not with a list of channel types. Class time goes to prediction and interpretation: think-pair-share on a recording trace, small-group data analysis, and quick polls that show me what the room actually believes before I move on.
  • Pair frequent low-stakes checks with comprehensive assessment. The students most likely to withdraw are those who discover in week ten that they misunderstood week three. Low-stakes quizzes, scaffolded labs, and collaborative problem-solving surface that while it is still easy to repair. At Daytona State, teaching anatomy and physiology to neurodiverse and multilingual students, this multimodal approach built technical skill and confidence together.
  • Design for access from the syllabus outward. Clear rubrics and checklists shared before the assignment, predictable course organization, accessible slides and captioned media, multiple ways to engage, and several valid modes of demonstrating understanding: written, oral, and visual. Building asynchronous courses for Oregon State's Ecampus has made me quicker at producing materials that are accessible by default rather than on request.
  • Put real data in students' hands early. In Molecular Genetics at San Francisco State, students analyzed authentic datasets, worked gene-editing case studies, and designed original experiments connecting lecture material to open questions. At Flagler, environmental science students sampled biodiversity in local ecosystems. Calcium-imaging recordings and transcriptomic datasets from my own lab become classroom material: quantify excitability under a defined perturbation, then propose the follow-up experiment.
  • Be explicit about how science gets done. Figure reading, peer review, the norms of authorship, brief metacognitive reflections on study strategy. Students who did not grow up around scientists are otherwise guessing at rules everyone else seems to know already.

§ 5

Mentoring and undergraduate research

My mentoring emphasizes independent scientific thinking and steady skill building. I structure research experiences as a progression from guided analyses to independent projects with clear paths to authorship or presentation: regular one-on-one meetings, near-peer mentoring in the lab, and collaborative figure and manuscript preparation. I see mentorship as a technical and intellectual partnership that also acknowledges students' lives outside the lab. A student who stops coming to lab in week five usually has a reason that has nothing to do with biology, and finding out is part of the job.

  • Crook Lab, San Francisco State University, 2022 to present. Trained master's and undergraduate students in molecular biology, microscopy, image quantification, and scientific communication. Mentees are co-authors on two of my papers, including three students on the intramuscular nerve cord manuscript. Master's thesis committee member.
  • Moroz Lab NSF REU cohorts, University of Florida, 2013 to 2018. Designed projects sized to a ten-week residency; led bioethics courses and symposium workshops. Mentee Rayanna Laux won the NSF REU Poster Presentation Award in 2015.
  • Academic advising. Instructor of record for San Francisco State's biology major-advising course, in which students build degree plans and career pathways.

A student who has recorded from a neuron they can name, under a condition they chose themselves, arrives at graduate or medical school already knowing how science is done.

§ 6

Curriculum development and professional development

I have developed course-embedded undergraduate research experiences and modular lab units linking foundational concepts to advanced neuroscience, and designed online-ready materials, mixed media, asynchronous discussion protocols, and reflective assessments, that I have since carried back into in-person courses.
