In brief

Ph.D. in neuroscience, University of Florida (Whitney Laboratory for Marine Bioscience). M.S., Cal Poly San Luis Obispo. B.S., UC Davis.

Seven peer-reviewed papers, three as first author, including Current Biology (2024) and iScience (2026).

Instructor of record at four institutions. Mentor to NSF REU, undergraduate, and master's students, several now co-authors.

Two-thirds of an octopus's half-billion neurons are not in its brain. They are in its arms, strung along a nerve cord that can plan and execute coordinated movement on its own. I want to know how that peripheral nervous system is organized, molecule by molecule and cell by cell, and how its circuits behave in real time.

During my postdoctoral work in Robyn Crook's laboratory at San Francisco State University, I produced the first three-dimensional molecular atlas of the octopus arm's axial nerve cord and then built calcium-imaging methods to watch its neurons respond, in populations, to the transmitters the atlas had mapped. That pairing of molecular identity with live physiology is the through-line of everything I do. It now points at a larger question: cephalopods live in coastal water where temperature and acidity swing on every tide, so what keeps circuits like these signaling under conditions that make other systems fail?

I came to research by way of teaching, and I have never separated the two. I have been instructor of record at four institutions, from gateway anatomy and physiology to upper-division molecular genetics, and I design my research so that undergraduates can do the science rather than assist with it. I am applying for faculty positions where research and mentorship are the same activity.
